Which training treats for your dog? The comparison

🐕 Dog & puppy · Buying guide · 7 min read

In kind, reward-based training, treats are your best tool: they tell the dog "that's exactly it!". But not all treats are equal. One that's too big fills them up, one that's too hard distracts them, one that's too ordinary won't motivate them outdoors. Here's how to choose.

In short: for training, choose treats that are small, soft and very tasty, which your dog swallows in a second. Have two levels: simple treats for home, and smellier "jackpot" treats for outdoors and difficult exercises.

What to look for

  • Size: a training treat should be tiny, about the size of a pea (even smaller for a small dog). You'll give lots during a session.
  • Texture: soft, so they swallow it quickly without long chewing or crumbs. Easy to break with your fingers.
  • Value: your dog decides what they like best. Try several kinds and rank them: keep the best for difficult moments (recall, walks with distractions).
  • Ingredients: read the label. Prefer a short ingredient list with meat or fish first, and avoid very sugary treats.
  • Practicality: not greasy to the touch and not crumbly, so they don't make a mess of the pouch.

1. Soft training treats

The basic choice: small tender pieces, often pre-cut, which you can break in two again. The dog swallows them without stopping, and you can do many repetitions. Close the bag properly so they don't harden.

2. Dried meat or fish

Chicken or duck strips, pieces of fish: very smelly, they motivate even dogs who aren't very food-driven. Cut them into tiny pieces before the session. They make good "jackpot" treats for outdoors.

3. Freeze-dried treats

Freeze-drying removes the water from meat or fish: the treat stays very smelly and very light. It crumbles easily, so you can make tiny rewards. Handy for dogs who turn their nose up at classic treats.

4. Paste in a tube

The dog licks the paste straight from the tip of the tube. The reward lasts a little longer, which is useful for rewarding calm: stay, brushing, nail trimming, a vet exam. It also means you don't touch the food with your fingers.

5. Hypoallergenic treats

If your dog has a food allergy or intolerance, or is on a prescribed diet, treats must be compatible: ask your vet. There are single-protein and hypoallergenic treats, and some veterinary diet brands offer treats suited to their diets.

What about homemade treats?

Small pieces of plain cooked chicken breast, with no salt or seasoning, make excellent, much-loved rewards. Keep them in the fridge and use them quickly. You can also use part of their kibble ration for easy exercises at home.

Portion control to avoid weight gain

Vets generally advise that treats should not exceed about 10% of what the dog eats in a day. In practice: make tiny pieces, and slightly reduce their meal on days with long sessions. For an overweight dog, talk to your vet.

Avoid: big chews during a session (too long to eat), very sugary biscuits, and above all foods that are toxic to dogs, such as chocolate, grapes, onion, garlic or products containing xylitol. If your dog eats any, call your vet.
Key points: small, soft and very tasty. A simple treat for home, a "jackpot" for outdoors, paste in a tube to reward calm. And always tiny pieces to keep them trim.
