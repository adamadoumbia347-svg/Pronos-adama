Teaching your dog "sit", "down" and "stay"

🐕 Dog & puppy · Beginner level · 7 min read

"Sit", "down" and "stay" are the basics of life together: waiting before crossing the road, staying calm while you open the door, being patient in front of the food bowl. Good news: these three exercises are easy to learn at any age, with treats and a few minutes a day. Here is the method, step by step, without ever forcing.

Before you start: the right gear and the right moment

  • Very small, tasty treats: pea-sized, so you can give lots without filling them up. Remember to take them out of their daily food.
  • A treat pouch on your belt: you reward at the right moment, without digging in your pockets.
  • A quiet place: start at home, with no distractions.
  • Short sessions: 3 to 5 minutes, several times a day, and always stop on a success.

"Sit": with a lure, in a few sessions

We use luring: the treat guides the dog into position, without ever touching them or pushing on their back.

  1. Hold a treat in front of their nose, then slowly raise it above their head, towards the back.
  2. To follow the treat with their eyes, they lift their head… and their bottom naturally goes down.
  3. The instant they sit, say "yes!" (or click if you use a clicker) and give the treat.
  4. After 5 to 10 successes, make the same gesture with no treat in your hand, and reward with a treat from the pouch.
  5. Add the word "sit" just before the gesture, when you're sure they'll sit. Little by little, the gesture becomes more discreet.

If they back up instead of sitting: the treat is too high. Keep it very close to their nose, or practise with their back to a wall.

"Down": from the sit

  1. Your dog is sitting. Place a treat against their nose and lower it slowly, straight down, between their front paws.
  2. Then move it gently forward, along the floor: they lie down to follow it.
  3. As soon as their elbows touch the floor, "yes!" and treat, given on the floor between their paws.
  4. As with the sit, remove the treat from your hand, then add the word "down".

"Down" is harder for some dogs, especially on a cold or slippery floor, or if they feel a bit unsure. Practise on a mat, and at first reward even a half-down: this is shaping, rewarding each small step forward.

"Stay": the "3 Ds", one at a time

"Stay" asks the dog to keep their position until you release them. To make it solid, we work on three criteria, which trainers call the 3 Ds: duration, distance and distractions. The golden rule: only increase one at a time.

  1. Choose a release word ("okay", "free", "go"): it means "you can move". Without it, the dog doesn't know when the exercise ends.
  2. Duration: dog sitting, count 1 second, reward while they are sitting, then release them. Build up gradually: 2 seconds, 3, 5, 10…
  3. Distance: go back to a short duration and take a single step back, then return to reward. Then two steps, three steps…
  4. Distractions: finally, somewhere easy, add small distractions (crouching, dropping a toy). Reduce duration and distance at that point.

Always go back to your dog to reward them rather than calling them: otherwise they learn that "stay" ends with running to you.

If they move: it's not a failure

If they get up before the release word, don't scold them. Calmly put them back in position and make the exercise easier (less time, less distance). A mistake simply means you went a little too fast. Aim for success on almost every try: that's what keeps them motivated.

Avoid: pushing on their back or pulling the lead to force them to sit or lie down, repeating the cue several times ("sit, sit, SIT"), or punishing them when they get it wrong. They learn faster, and with pleasure, when you reward what they do well.

Using it in real life

A dog who sits perfectly in the living room won't necessarily do it at the park: dogs don't generalise well. Then practise in other rooms, in the garden, then on walks, going back to very easy exercises in each new place. Use these positions every day: "sit" before you put the bowl down, "stay" before opening the door, "down" at a café.

Little by little, replace some treats with other rewards: a stroke, a game, permission to go and sniff. Still give treats from time to time: they keep motivation going.

What about a puppy?

You can start as soon as they arrive, with even shorter sessions (1 to 2 minutes) and lots of play. A puppy tires quickly and concentrates for short periods: that's normal. If your puppy or dog struggles to sit or lie down, or seems uncomfortable, talk to your vet: pain can make these positions unpleasant.

Key points: luring for sit and down, then remove the treat from your hand and add the word. For stay, a release word and the 3 Ds (duration, distance, distractions), one at a time. Short sessions, frequent successes, never force.
