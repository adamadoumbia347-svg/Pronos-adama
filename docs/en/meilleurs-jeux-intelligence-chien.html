Best Puzzle Toys for Dogs: Our Comparison

🧩 Dog · Buying guide · 6 min read · Updated November 2, 2026

A dog who thinks is often a calmer dog. Puzzle toys make your dog search for food with their nose and paws, and tire their mind as much as a walk. But between puzzles, mats and dispensers, which should you choose? Here's our comparison, by level and personality.

In short: to start, an easy puzzle or a snuffle mat. For a dog who has got the idea, a higher-level puzzle to play together. Puzzles with removable parts are for supervised play.

What to look for

  • The level: a game that's too hard discourages. Start easy so your dog succeeds quickly.
  • Sturdiness: a chewer needs a robust model, with no small parts to swallow.
  • Supervision: most puzzles are played together, not when your dog is alone.
  • Cleaning: washable plastic or machine-washable fabric.
  • Size: suited to their muzzle and paws.

1. The easy puzzle: to get started

Compartments with lids or cones to lift, hiding a treat. Your dog learns that by pushing or lifting, they get a reward. It's the best starting point, including for puppies and older dogs.

2. The levelled puzzle: to progress

Sliders, drawers and levers to combine: these games need several moves in the right order. Play them together, and they work beautifully with clicker training: click every good idea. Put them away afterwards, as removable parts can be chewed.

5. The hide-and-seek plush

A large plush (a tree trunk, a house) with small plush toys hidden inside for your dog to pull out. It's a search game that satisfies the urge to "unstuff" without wrecking your cushions. Best for dogs who don't shred everything in minutes.

Which game for which dog?

  • Puppy: an easy puzzle or a snuffle mat, in very short sessions.
  • Very active or working breed: levelled puzzles and scent games, on top of real activities. Calm after the session is a skill too.
  • Fast eater: a treat dispenser or snuffle mat to slow meals down.
  • Senior dog: easy games, no physical effort, that keep them curious.
  • Anxious dog: the snuffle mat, since sniffing is calming. Avoid noisy games.

No budget for a puzzle? Hide some kibble in a cardboard egg box or roll it up in an old towel: a great first brain game, always supervised.

Puzzle toys or enrichment toys?

Puzzle toys are mostly played with you. For a dog who has to keep busy alone and safely, rubber stuffable toys are often better: see our guide to enrichment toys for a dog left alone.

Getting the most out of a puzzle

  • Show them the first time: let them watch you hide the treat.
  • Help them if they get stuck, rather than letting them give up.
  • Use their daily food to avoid weight gain.
  • Rotate games to keep interest.
Avoid: leaving a dog alone with a puzzle that has removable parts, starting with a game that's too hard, or scolding a dog who can't do it. The goal is for them to enjoy searching.
Key points: start with an easy puzzle or a snuffle mat, increase the difficulty once they've got it, play together and put fragile games away after the session.
