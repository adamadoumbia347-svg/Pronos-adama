Best Toys for Indoor Cats: Which to Choose?

🪶 Cat · Buying guide · 6 min read · Updated November 2, 2026

An indoor cat is a hunter without a hunting ground. Without the right games, they can get bored, put on weight, wake the whole house at night or ambush your ankles. Good toys imitate prey and let your cat stalk, chase and catch. Here's which to choose.

In short: the must-have is the feather wand, for hunting sessions with you. Add small light toys for solo play, a puzzle feeder to search for kibble, and rotate toys to keep them interesting.

What to look for

  • Prey-like movement: a toy that moves, hides, darts and stops, like a mouse or a bird.
  • Safety: no strings, ribbons or small parts left out when you're not there.
  • Play with you and alone: your cat needs both.
  • Variety: a cat soon tires of a toy that's always out.

1. The feather wand: the essential

It's the toy that comes closest to a real hunt. Move it like prey: hide it behind furniture, dart it along the floor, stop, then fly it up. Let your cat catch it regularly, and end the session with a catch followed by a small meal or treat: the full sequence of stalk, chase, catch and eat helps them settle.

Put it away after play: the string can be dangerous if chewed or swallowed.

2. Light balls and mice

Foam balls, fabric mice, bell balls: light enough to skitter at the slightest tap. Keep a few out and put the rest away to bring back later.

3. The puzzle feeder

A board or ball from which your cat fishes out kibble with a paw. It recreates the effort of hunting and slows meals down. Start with an easy version, and show them the first time.

5. Track toys and 6. catnip

A ball track lets your cat play alone without the ball vanishing under the sofa. Catnip (or valerian) toys attract many cats, but not all: some don't react at all. Offer them now and then so they keep their effect.

Which toy for which cat?

  • Kitten: feather wand and small balls, in short sessions. Now's the time to teach them to play with toys, not hands.
  • Active adult: several wand sessions a day, a tunnel and a puzzle feeder.
  • Greedy or overweight cat: a puzzle feeder, so they work for part of their food.
  • Senior cat: calmer games on the floor, with the wand moved slowly. They still love to hunt, at their own pace.
  • Shy cat: start playing from a distance with a long wand, so they dare to come closer.

Toys don't replace an indoor cat's other needs: scratching posts, high perches, hideouts and a window with a view all enrich their day.

Playing well with your cat

  • Several short sessions a day, especially in the evening, rather than one long one.
  • Rotate toys: put some away and bring them back later.
  • Never use your hands as toys: your cat would learn to attack them.
  • A laser pointer on its own is frustrating as they can never catch it: if you use one, always finish on a real toy or treat to catch.
  • Try clicker training: many cats love learning little tricks, see our clicker training guide.
Avoid: leaving strings, hair ties, ribbons or small parts out, playing with your hands, or always leaving the same toys on the floor. A cat whose behaviour suddenly changes (no play, no appetite, hiding) should see a vet.
Key points: a feather wand to hunt with you, a few light toys for solo play, a puzzle feeder for kibble, and regular rotation. That's the key to a happy indoor cat.
