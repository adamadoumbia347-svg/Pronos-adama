Which dog harness should you choose? Our comparison

🐕 Dog · Buying guide · 7 min read · Updated October 16, 2026

A good harness protects your dog's neck, makes walks more pleasant and helps with loose-lead training. But between Y-front, no-pull, H-shaped and “Norwegian” harnesses, it's hard to know where to start. Here's our comparison to help you choose.

In short: for most dogs, a Y-front harness with two rings (one on the back, one on the chest) is the best choice: it leaves the shoulders free and helps your dog learn not to pull.

Why a harness rather than a collar?

When a dog pulls on a collar, all the pressure goes on the throat and neck, a delicate area (windpipe, thyroid, neck vertebrae). A harness spreads that pressure across the chest. This matters most for dogs that still pull, small dogs, flat-faced breeds and dogs with breathing problems. A collar is still useful for carrying the ID tag.

1. The Y-front harness: the best everyday choice

Seen from the front, its straps form a “Y” that runs between the front legs and clears the shoulders. Your dog keeps a natural stride, which makes it ideal for long walks, canicross or hiking.

2. The two-clip harness: for learning not to pull

This is a Y-front harness with a second ring on the chest. With the lead clipped at the front, a dog that pulls is gently turned back towards you instead of moving forwards: a real help while you work on loose-lead walking, without any pain. The Ruffwear Front Range is a well-known model of this type; many other brands make an equivalent.

3. The H-harness: for escape artists

It has one strap around the neck and another behind the front legs, joined by a band along the back. Some models add a third strap around the belly, which makes them very hard to back out of: a real plus for nervous dogs, newly adopted dogs or rescues.

4. The horizontal-strap harness: handy, but not ideal

Very common, it goes on in seconds. But its horizontal strap sits across the front of the shoulders and can limit movement, especially if the dog pulls. Keep it for dogs that already walk nicely on the lead, or for short outings.

5. The car safety harness

In the car, a dog should be secured or travel in a crate. A harness that works with the seat belt stops your dog being thrown forward when you brake. Choose a model designed for this purpose rather than a walking harness clipped to the belt.

How to choose the right size

  1. Measure the chest girth at its widest point, just behind the front legs.
  2. Measure the neck at its base, where a collar would sit.
  3. Compare with the manufacturer's size guide (it varies from brand to brand); if you're between two sizes, choose the one with more room to adjust.
  4. Once it's on, you should be able to slide two fingers under each strap, and the harness mustn't rub under the armpits.

Getting your dog used to the harness

Some dogs don't like having something put over their head. Go gently with positive reinforcement: show the harness, reward; let your dog put its head through the opening by holding a treat on the other side; close one buckle, reward. A few sessions are often enough. This is the principle of cooperative care.

Avoid: choke, prong or electric collars sold as “anti-pull”. They work through pain and can cause injury. Why we advise against them. A harness that's too tight, rubs or leaves marks doesn't fit properly: loosen it or change the size.
Key points: a Y-front harness with two clips suits the vast majority of dogs. Measure before you buy, check the two-finger rule, and combine the harness with real loose-lead training.
