Clicker Training for Beginners: Dogs and Cats

🔘 Dog & cat · Beginner level · 7 min read · Updated November 2, 2026

A little "click", a reward, and your pet knows exactly what they did right. Clicker training is one of the clearest and most enjoyable ways to learn, for dogs and cats alike. Here's how to get started, step by step, even if you've never held a clicker.

In short: the click marks the exact moment of the right behaviour and announces a reward. Start by "charging" the clicker (click = treat), then click as soon as your pet does what you want. Sessions of a few minutes, a reward after every click, and never any punishment.

How does a clicker work?

A clicker is a small device that always makes the same sound. That sound is a marker: it tells your pet "yes, exactly that!" at the precise moment they act. A treat follows right after. Because the click is faster and clearer than a word, your pet understands better what earned the reward.

It's a form of positive reinforcement: you reward what you want to see again. You'll find more detail on our clicker page.

Before you start: get your treats ready

  • Tiny treats your pet can swallow in a second: you'll give a lot. Take them out of the day's food.
  • A treat they love: it's what gives the click its value.
  • A quiet place with no distractions, and a pet that's neither starving nor full.
  • A treat pouch so the treats are always to hand: timing matters.

Step 1: "charge" the clicker

At first, the click means nothing to your pet. You need to pair it with the reward:

  1. Click once, then give a treat straight away. Your pet doesn't have to do anything.
  2. Repeat about ten times, varying the moment a little.
  3. Test it: click when they're looking away. If they quickly turn to you or to the treat, it worked: the click now means something.

If your pet jumps at the sound, hold the clicker further away, click inside your pocket, or use an adjustable-volume clicker.

Step 2: an easy first exercise, touching your hand

Targeting is a great first exercise for dogs and cats:

  1. Hold your open hand a few centimetres from their nose.
  2. Out of curiosity, they'll sniff it: the moment their nose touches your hand, click, then treat.
  3. Repeat, moving your hand a little. When they touch it every time, add a word ("touch") just before offering your hand.

This little trick is useful for everything: moving your pet without pulling, getting them onto the scales, calling them to your side.

Step 3: capturing and shaping

  • Capturing: click a behaviour they already do on their own, like sitting or lying down. They quickly learn they can "make" the click happen (see capturing).
  • Shaping: for a more complex behaviour, click each small step towards it, then gradually ask for more (see shaping).

That's how you can teach "sit", "down" and "stay", as well as tricks and useful behaviours for care.

The golden rules

  • One click = one reward, always, even if you clicked a little early.
  • The click ends the behaviour: it's normal for your pet to move after the click.
  • Short sessions: a few minutes, and stop on a success.
  • Don't use the clicker to call your pet or to get their attention.
  • No empty clicks: never click without rewarding, or the click loses its value.

What about cats?

Cats learn very well with a clicker! Use a very tasty treat (paste, a piece of cooked chicken), even shorter sessions, and a soft-sounding clicker if your cat is sensitive. Useful first exercises: touching your hand, coming when called, going into the carrier, hopping onto a stool. Many indoor cats love these sessions as much as play: add some good toys for indoor cats too.

Common mistakes

Avoid: clicking too late (after the behaviour), forgetting the reward, sessions that are too long, raising the difficulty too fast, or getting frustrated. If your pet loses interest, make the exercise easier so they succeed, then end the session on a win.

What next?

Once a behaviour is reliable and linked to a word, you can gradually drop the clicker for that behaviour and reward it in other ways (praise, play, an occasional treat). Keep the clicker for new learning. To keep your dog's mind busy between sessions, puzzle toys are a great addition.

Key points: charge the clicker first, click at the exact moment of the right behaviour, reward after every click, keep sessions short and end on a success. Clicker training is a game for both of you.
