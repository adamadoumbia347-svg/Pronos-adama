Cat peeing outside the litter box: causes and solutions

🐈 Cat · Behaviour problem · 7 min read · Updated October 23, 2026

A puddle on the rug, a smell on the bed, and the feeling that your cat is doing it "on purpose". Don't worry: cats don't take revenge. When a cat pees outside the box, they're telling you something is wrong: with their health, their litter or their surroundings. Here's how to find the cause and help them, without ever punishing them.

Emergency: if your cat keeps going to the box without managing to pee, strains, cries while peeing or has blood in their urine, call your vet the same day. In male cats especially, a urinary blockage can become life-threatening within hours.

Step 1: rule out a health problem

This is the first thing to check, before changing anything about the litter. Cystitis, bladder stones, an infection, diabetes, kidney problems or joint pain (which makes getting into the box hard for an older cat) can all cause peeing outside the box. A cat sometimes links the litter box with pain and then avoids it. A check-up with your vet, often with a urine test, will clarify things.

Step 2: peeing or marking?

  • "Normal" peeing: a fairly large puddle on a horizontal surface (rug, duvet, laundry, floor). The cat is avoiding the box for a specific reason.
  • Urine marking: a small spray on a vertical surface (wall, curtain, door), with the cat standing and their raised tail quivering. It's a message linked to territory or stress.

Marking is much more common in cats that aren't neutered: talk to your vet about neutering if it hasn't been done yet.

Step 3: check the box and the litter

Cats are very particular about their toilet. The most common causes:

  • A box that isn't clean enough: scoop poo and urine at least once a day, and replace all the litter regularly.
  • Not enough boxes: the usual advice is one box per cat, plus one. In a house with several floors, have one on each level.
  • A box that's too small: it should be about one and a half times your cat's length, not counting the tail.
  • A bad location: next to the washing machine, in a busy hallway or by their food bowl. Choose a quiet corner where they can see others coming.
  • Litter they don't like: scented, too coarse under their paws, or changed suddenly. Many cats prefer fine, unscented litter. See our cat litter comparison.
  • A covered box: it traps smells inside and some cats feel cornered. Try an open box next to it.

Step 4: spot sources of stress

Moving house, building work, a new cat, a baby, a neighbour's cat visible through the window or tension between cats in the home can all lead to peeing outside the box or marking. To help:

  • keep a steady routine (meals, play and cuddles at the same times);
  • give them hiding places and high spots;
  • in a multi-cat home, provide more of everything (boxes, bowls, scratching posts, beds) in different rooms;
  • if an outdoor cat worries them, cover the lower part of the windows.

A feline pheromone diffuser can help some cats feel safer, alongside these changes.

Step 5: clean soiled spots properly

As long as they can smell urine, your cat may come back to the same place. Use an enzyme cleaner, which actually breaks down the smelly molecules. Avoid bleach and ammonia-based products: their smell resembles urine and can encourage them to go there again.

Then make the spot less appealing: temporarily put their food bowl, a bed or a toy there (cats avoid peeing where they eat and sleep), or, if it's always the same corner, put an extra box there.

Avoid: telling them off, rubbing their nose in it or punishing them. They don't make the connection, and stress almost always makes the problem worse. They might also start hiding to pee.
Key points: the vet first, then a clean, large, well-placed box, fine unscented litter, enzyme cleaning and less stress. If the problem continues, your vet can refer you to a veterinary behaviourist.
